Keeping Time with the Skies

Have you ever spotted the Moon during the daytime? Why do festivals like Diwali and Holi fall on different dates each year? How did ancient people measure months and years without clocks?

In this chapter we discover how the Sun, Moon and stars helped humans keep time — and how we still use their cycles today.

Slide 2 · The base unit of time

Day and Night — Earth’s Rotation

Earth spins on its axis once every 24 hours. The side facing the Sun has day; the opposite side has night.

Rotation speed1×

Key idea

Earth rotates from west to east. The Sun appears to rise in the east and set in the west because of this spin.

One full rotation relative to the Sun takes about 24 hours — that is the length of a day.

Local context

Sunrise and sunset times shift slightly through the year across India. In June, days are longer in the north; in December they are shorter.

Slide 3 · The month

Phases of the Moon

As the Moon revolves around Earth, the portion of its sunlit face that we can see changes. This cycle takes about 29.5 days.

Moon position in orbit0°

New Moon (Amavasya)

The Moon is between Earth and the Sun. The dark side faces us — we see almost no illuminated portion.

Remember

Phases are not caused by Earth’s shadow. Earth’s shadow causes a lunar eclipse — a different event.

Slide 4 · Shukla & Krishna Paksha

Waxing and Waning

The bright part of the Moon grows (Shukla Paksha) and then shrinks (Krishna Paksha).

Shukla Paksha · Waxing

The illuminated portion grows larger each night.

  • Starts after Amavasya (New Moon)
  • Ends at Purnima (Full Moon)
  • “Shukla” means bright / white
  • Moon stays visible longer after sunset

Krishna Paksha · Waning

The illuminated portion shrinks each night.

  • Starts after Purnima (Full Moon)
  • Ends at Amavasya (New Moon)
  • “Krishna” means dark
  • Moon is mainly visible in the pre-dawn sky

Slide 5 · Why the delay?

Why the Moon Rises ~50 Minutes Later Each Day

While Earth rotates once in 24 hours, the Moon is also moving ahead in its orbit.

The key idea

After Earth completes one full spin, the Moon has already shifted eastward. Earth must rotate a little extra (~12–13°) to catch up.

That extra rotation takes roughly ~50 minutes.

Slide 6 · Earth’s revolution

Measuring a Year — Seasons

Earth takes about 365.25 days to complete one full orbit around the Sun. The tilt of Earth’s axis creates the seasons.

Position in orbit0°

Northern Spring

Earth’s axis tilt of ~23.5° means different parts of the planet receive more direct sunlight at different times of the year.

India’s seasons

When the Northern Hemisphere tilts toward the Sun → summer. When it tilts away → winter. The monsoon and harvest cycles follow these changes.

Slide 7 · Ways of counting time

Solar, Lunar & Luni-Solar Calendars

Different cultures track time using the Sun, the Moon, or a combination of both.

Solar

Gregorian calendar

Based on Earth’s orbit around the Sun. 365 / 366 days. Used worldwide for civil dates.

Lunar

Pure Moon cycle

Based only on Moon phases. ~29.5-day months. Islamic Hijri calendar is an example.

Luni-Solar

Indian National Calendar

Combines Moon months with the solar year. Begins with Chaitra. Used for most Indian festivals.

Slide 10 · Wrap-up

Summary & Home Activity

Key takeaways

  • Day = Earth’s rotation (~24 h)
  • Month ≈ Moon’s revolution (~29.5 d)
  • Year = Earth’s revolution around the Sun
  • Phases = changing view of the sunlit Moon (not Earth’s shadow)
  • Indian festivals follow the luni-solar calendar
  • Satellites extend our eyes into space
